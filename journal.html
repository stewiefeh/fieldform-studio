The hero stair, retired.

Every practice has a project type it quietly refuses now. Ours is the "hero stair": the floating, sculptural, magazine-cover staircase that eats a quarter of a house's circulation budget and gets photographed from below, once, on opening day.

We built three of them between 2016 and 2019. The verdict after living with the owners for a few years is consistent. Nobody uses the word "hero" by month six. What they notice is the handrail that never got resolved, the heat that pool-fountains up through the open volume, and the six square meters of dead double-height space that has to be dusted. Meanwhile the actual daily stair, the back one, is where life happens, and in all three houses it is a straight flight we drew in an afternoon.

The replacement idea is embarrassingly simple: spend the stair budget on the stair people use, and give the saved space to a room. Cass Terrace has a version of this, a generous oak flight with a south window that costs a tenth of a cantilevered showpiece. It gets photographed from the landing, with coffee cups in frame, and that is the better picture.

September 2026 · Tomas Lindqvist

Concrete, honestly.

Concrete carries a carbon budget of roughly one ton of CO2 per ton of cement, and architecture spends that budget with the discipline of a lottery winner. So here is the honest ledger we run at Fieldform before any pour.

Concrete earns its footprint where it does structural work nothing else can: spanning, cantilevering, holding earth, holding fire off a stair. It earns it again where its thermal mass is actually coupled to the interior, which means exposed soffits over occupied space, not a rendered skim over insulation that throws the benefit away. Ashgrove House passes both tests. Decorative board-formed feature walls in a heated space fail both, and we no longer draw them.

Where it fails, we specify mass timber, rammed earth, or plain old wood frame, depending on span and fire strategy. Clients sometimes read this as anti-concrete. It is the opposite: refusing to spend concrete on pageantry is what lets us use it where it is unmatched.

June 2026 · Priya Raman

The case for smaller rooms.

The best room in Hollowell Barn is 2.4 meters wide. It is the writing room, and it fits exactly one desk, one chair, one window, and the person using them. Tenants fight over it. The 40-square-meter living room below sits empty by comparison most evenings.

There is a scale at which a room stops being a room and becomes an address. Past about 5.5 meters of unsupported width, a domestic space stops inviting the small postures, reading, mending, talking sideways, and starts demanding furniture arranged for company that is not there. Clients call this "flexible open plan." The cat knows better: it sits under the desk in the small room.

So our residential plans now budget enclosure like a material. Every house gets at least one room small enough to hold a conversation at a whisper and one big enough for a crowd, and neither pretends to be the other. It costs nothing at sketch stage and everything later, which is the only trade worth making early.

April 2026 · Adam Reyes

What a 12-week feasibility gets you.

A developer client once told us our feasibility studies were "expensive homework." Then he wired us for four more, because each one saved him from buying, or taught him he could buy more, than the brochure promised. Both outcomes pay for the study several times over.

The deliverable is short on purpose: zoning capacity in buildable square meters, two or three massing options with cost bands attached, a code and heritage screen with the risks named, and a written go or no-go. No renders, because a render is a promise and a study is a question.

The number people undervalue is the no-go. Half our studies end there, and every one of those saved a client from a two-year mistake. We put the recommendation in writing, and the contract says the study ends there, no obligation to continue. A feasibility you cannot walk away from is a sales funnel, not a study.
